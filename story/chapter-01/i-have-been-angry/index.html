Prologue

I have been angry for as long as I can remember.

2026-09-29

I have been angry for as long as I can remember.

Not the loud kind of angry all the time. Not the kind that punches walls or wakes up every morning looking for somebody to fight. Mine has always been more attentive than that.

I notice things.

I notice the hesitation in somebody’s voice when the story they are telling is not quite true. I notice when the person with the most authority in the room is also the least qualified to have it. I notice when somebody is being humiliated and everyone else decides, for reasons I have never understood, that looking away is the more civilized thing to do.

I notice hypocrisy. Cruelty. Manipulation.

The small abuses people excuse because confronting them would be inconvenient.

And once I see something, I have never been particularly good at pretending I didn’t.

That has caused problems.

A lot of them.

Enough problems, in fact, that a reasonable person might assume I would have learned my lesson by now.

Apparently, I am not a reasonable person.

Because I still do not know what the lesson is supposed to be.

Stay quiet?

Mind my own business?

Watch someone treat another human being like garbage and convince myself that silence is maturity?

Learn to smile politely at liars because pointing out the lie makes everybody uncomfortable?

I have tried to understand that logic.

I still don’t.

My anger has never been especially selective.

I hate irresponsible fathers. I hate abusive mothers. I hate people who walk into every room already convinced they are the smartest person in it. People who believe the rules are for everybody except them. People who spend their lives mistreating everyone around them and somehow remain permanently astonished that life keeps happening to them.

Liars. Cheaters. Manipulators. Gaslighters.

People who demand sympathy for wounds they inflicted themselves.

It makes very little difference to me what a person looks like, how old they are, where they come from, what they believe, or what title they carry.

If something is wrong, it is wrong.

And unfortunately for me—and occasionally for everybody standing nearby—I have rarely possessed the ability to leave it at that.

I say something.

Sometimes I probably should not.

Sometimes I absolutely should.

The problem is that, in the moment, I usually cannot tell the difference.

This has not made me particularly good at collecting people.

There are people I have liked, of course. Good people, or at least people who seemed good in the beginning.

Hard workers. Decent neighbors. Funny coworkers. People who appeared to have survived adulthood without becoming completely unbearable.

And for a while, sometimes a long while, I would think:

Maybe this one is different.

Then eventually something would happen.

A comment. A lie. A betrayal. The way they treated a waiter. The way they spoke to someone they supposedly loved. The way they spoke about someone weaker than them. The way they suddenly changed when money entered the room.

And there it was.

That thing.

The thing I could not unsee.

The thing everybody else seemed capable of overlooking.

I have spent much of my life wondering whether the problem was the world or me.

There is a certain arrogance in believing you are surrounded by people who constantly disappoint you. I understand that. Eventually, if you find something wrong with almost everybody, an obvious question begins to form:

What if you are the problem?

It is a fair question.

I have asked it many times.

I still ask it.

But there is another part of this story.

A much smaller group of people exists in my life.

Very small.

Small enough that I could probably count them without much effort.

And I love them with a ferocity that makes almost everything else I have just written seem insignificant.

I waited most of my life for them.

I did not know I was waiting for them.

I did not know what their faces would look like, what their voices would sound like, which one would inherit which part of me, or how completely they would rearrange the architecture of my existence.

I only know that eventually, they arrived.

My children.

And sometimes—usually when I am thinking about the things I would rather forget—I wonder whether there was a purpose hidden inside all of it.

The humiliations. The injustices. The abuse.

The situations that made me angry before I was old enough to understand exactly what anger was.

The adults who should have known better.

The people who failed me.

The things I saw.

The things I survived.

The things I promised myself I would never become.

Maybe none of it happened for a reason. Maybe that is simply something human beings tell themselves because randomness is harder to live with.

I don’t know.

But I do know this:

By the time my children came into my life, I had spent years collecting examples of the kind of father I never wanted to be.

I had been studying the subject without knowing there would ever be an exam.

And then, one day, there was.

Them.

Perhaps that is where this story really begins.

Or perhaps it began decades earlier, with a child who was already watching everything around him, already keeping score, already learning that adults could be wrong, authority could be undeserved, love could come with conditions, and silence could sometimes be its own kind of betrayal.

I am still trying to figure out exactly where the beginning is.

That is one of the reasons I am telling this story.

The other reason is much simpler.

My children finally arrived.

And someday, I want them to understand who was waiting for them.
